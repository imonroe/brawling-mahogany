The chain of dates that governs a live transaction — mutual acceptance, the
inspection objection deadline, the appraisal, loan commitment, closing. This is
the part of the product that earns its keep: get the chain right once, and
every screen that asks "what is coming" answers correctly for the rest of the
deal.

You will find it on a deal's **Dates** tab, and across every deal at
**Dates & Deadlines** in the sidebar.

## Two ways a date gets its day

**Type it in.** You read it off the contract and enter it. Simple, and the
right answer for anything the contract states outright.

**Count it from another date.** Ten calendar days after mutual acceptance;
three business days before closing. You choose the date it counts from, the
number of days, and whether they are calendar days or business days — and the
day is worked out for you.

The second kind is what makes the rest of this work.

> **Business days skip weekends and nothing else.** Public holidays are not
> counted, because a holiday calendar means a jurisdiction, an observance rule,
> and a yearly maintenance job we would eventually get wrong without telling
> you. If a contract counts holidays, type the day in.

## When a date moves

One always does. Closing slips by three days, and everything counted from it
should slip too.

It does — and you see what will move **before** it moves. Change a date and the
form shows you every other date that shifts, with its old day and its new one.
Nothing is saved until you have seen that list.

If eleven dates move, you will be told eleven dates moved. The deal's activity
records it as one entry rather than eleven, so the change is findable later
without burying everything else that happened that week.

### Typing over a derived date detaches it

If you set a day by hand on a date that was counted from another one, it stops
following. The screen says so, and it still tells you what it used to follow —
"was 10 calendar days after Mutual acceptance, until somebody set it by hand" —
so a date that looks wrong six weeks later can be explained.

Give it an anchor again and it starts following again.

### Deleting a date leaves what followed it alone

Anything counted from the date you delete keeps the day it has. The obligation
in the contract did not disappear because you tidied up the calendar.

## Critical dates

Mark a date **critical** and it is announced more often and earlier — two weeks
out, a week, three days, the day before, and on the day itself. An ordinary
date gets a week's notice and the day before.

The day-of notice for a critical date is the one thing in this product that
ignores quiet hours. A missed inspection objection deadline is a legal problem,
and there is nothing to be done about today's deadline tomorrow morning.

**Remind this many days before** on the date itself sets its own schedule —
`14, 7, 1` on a financing contingency, `1` on a walkthrough. Leave it as it
comes and the date uses the default for its kind; clear the field and the
reminders stop.

Two things it does not switch off. A **critical** date still gets its notice on
the morning it falls — that is what the flag is for, and turning the warnings
down says how much notice you want, not that you would rather not know the day
has arrived. And nothing here changes the screens: the date is still on the
calendar, in the fourteen-day list, and on the deal.

## Reminders

Reminders arrive in the morning, in **your team's** timezone, and several
deadlines on one day arrive as **one** email rather than four. An email per
deadline is an email people filter.

Move a date and its reminders move with it, with nothing to reschedule: the
reminder is worked out each morning from where the date is now.

Close a deal and its reminders stop.

## Dates as a requirement

A stage can wait for a named date to arrive. Add a **Date reached** requirement
to a stage in your template and name the date — the same name your deals use
for it, capitals and spacing ignored — and the requirement is met from that day
onwards.

**It does not move the deal on by itself.** Nothing in this product advances a
stage without somebody pressing **Advance**; what the requirement does is stop
being the thing in the way. That is deliberate — an advance can send a client
an email, and an email nobody chose to send is not something you can take
back.

If a deal has no date of that name, the requirement says so and links to the
tab where you would add it.

## Dates that came from Extract

A later release will read dates off a contract. When it does, a date it
proposes is shown as **Suggested — not confirmed**, and it is counted nowhere
until somebody agrees to it: not in the fourteen-day list, not on the calendar,
not by a requirement, and not by a reminder. A proposal is not a deadline.

## What your client sees

Only dates that are still ahead, in plain language, on their status page. Never
a date that has passed, and never the word _overdue_. If something is late, you
handle it by phone.
